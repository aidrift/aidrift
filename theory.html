Theory of Drifting

A drift is not a loss of control — it is a controlled operating point that happens to be unstable. This page walks through the vehicle dynamics behind it: how a car's motion is described, where tire forces run out, what the front wheels are actually doing, what defines a drift equilibrium, and why a 1:43 model is a physically meaningful place to study it.

1 — Describing the motion

Vehicle dynamics is written in a frame attached to the car: x points forward along the chassis, y to the left, and the car rotates about the vertical z axis. In this body frame the planar motion of the vehicle is fully described by three numbers.

  • Longitudinal velocity vx — how fast the car moves along its own nose.
  • Lateral velocity vy — how fast it moves sideways. In normal driving this is nearly zero; in a drift it is large.
  • Yaw rate r — how fast the car rotates about its vertical axis.

The single most important derived quantity is the side-slip angle β — the angle between where the car is pointing and where it is actually going:

β = arctan( vy / vx ) Normal cornering: |β| below about 5°. Drifting: 20–60° and beyond. Side-slip is the number that separates "turning" from "drifting".

A car in a drift is therefore moving in one direction while aimed in another. Everything that follows — the tire forces, the equilibrium, the counter-steer — is a consequence of keeping that mismatch stable instead of letting it collapse into a spin.

2 — The single-track (bicycle) model

For planar dynamics the two wheels on each axle can be lumped into one. The result — the single-track or bicycle model — is the workhorse of vehicle dynamics: simple enough to analyze by hand, rich enough to predict real drifting behaviour.

Each axle's slip angle follows from the geometry above — the wheel travels with the body velocity plus the rotation of the chassis about the centre of gravity:

αf = arctan( (vy + a r) / vx ) − δ
αr = arctan( (vy − b r) / vx ) The yaw rate adds a·r of lateral velocity at the front axle and subtracts b·r at the rear — which is why a rotating car loads its two axles very differently.

Newton's and Euler's equations in the rotating body frame then give the three equations of motion. Note the vy r and vx r terms: they are not forces but the consequence of writing dynamics in a frame that itself rotates.

m( v̇x − vy r ) = Fxr + Fxf cos δ − Fyf sin δ
m( v̇y + vx r ) = Fyr + Fyf cos δ + Fxf sin δ
Iz ṙ = a( Fyf cos δ + Fxf sin δ ) − b Fyr Three states, three equations. Everything a drift controller does, it does by choosing δ and the drive force Fxr so that this system settles at the operating point we want.

3 — Where the grip runs out

The equations above are only as good as the tire forces fed into them. At small slip angles a tire behaves like a spring — lateral force grows in proportion to slip, with the constant of proportionality called cornering stiffness Cα:

Fy ≈ − Cα α   (small α only) This linear region is where ordinary driving lives, and where the classic understeer/oversteer analysis applies.

But grip is finite. As slip grows the force curve bends over, peaks, and then falls away. Beyond the peak the tire is saturated: asking for more slip returns less force, not more. That inversion is exactly what makes the far side of the curve treacherous — and it is where a drift lives.

Worse, lateral and longitudinal demands compete for the same limited friction. Their combination is bounded by the friction circle:

√( Fx² + Fy² ) ≤ μ Fz Every newton spent accelerating is a newton unavailable for cornering. Applying throttle to the rear axle deliberately shrinks its cornering capacity — this is how a drift is initiated and sustained.

4 — Steering geometry: Ackermann and its limits

The single-track model collapses each axle into one wheel. A real car has two, and in a turn they travel on circles of different radius — so they cannot point in the same direction without fighting each other. The fix is two centuries old: Ackermann steering geometry, patented by Georg Lankensperger in 1817 for horse-drawn carriages and named after Rudolph Ackermann, who filed it in England.

The principle: for every wheel to roll without lateral scrub, the axes of all four wheels must intersect at a single point — the instantaneous centre of rotation. Since the rear axle does not steer, that point always lies on the extension of the rear axle line, and the inner front wheel must be steered more than the outer one.

Writing that condition out gives the Ackermann relation between the inner and outer steering angles, the track width w and the wheelbase L:

cot δo − cot δi = w / L In a real car this is realised purely mechanically, by a steering trapezoid: the steering arms are angled inward toward the centre of the rear axle. No electronics involved — the geometry does the work.

Why racing cars deliberately get it "wrong"

Ackermann is derived from pure rolling — no slip angles anywhere. That assumption holds while parking, and fails progressively as speed rises. Two effects work against it:

  • Tires need slip angle to make force at all, so the "correct" geometric angle is no longer the angle that maximises grip.
  • Lateral load transfer puts far more vertical load on the outer tire. A more heavily loaded tire reaches its peak at a larger slip angle — so the outer wheel wants more steer, not less.

The result is that production cars implement only partial Ackermann, and many racing cars run parallel steering or invert it entirely into anti-Ackermann.

And in a drift, it barely matters

Every version of this argument assumes the car is roughly following where it points. In a drift at β = 40°, it is not. The whole vehicle is sliding, both front tires already carry large slip angles imposed by the chassis attitude, and the few degrees of difference between Ackermann and anti-Ackermann are lost in the noise.

What does matter to a drift car is far more basic:

  • Steering range. Holding a large side-slip requires large counter-steer. Purpose-built drift cars use modified knuckles to reach 60–70° of lock instead of the usual 35°.
  • Symmetry. Left and right lock must match, or the car behaves differently in each direction — an asymmetry a learning-based controller will happily absorb as a bias.
  • Resolution and repeatability. The controller commands small corrections continuously; backlash in the linkage is a bigger enemy than imperfect geometry.

What this means for AIDrift

At 1:43 scale with a single servo and a printed linkage, exact Ackermann is neither achievable nor worth chasing. The front end is designed for maximum symmetric lock and minimum slop instead — see the servo and linkage steps on the Platform page.

5 — What a drift actually is

Set every derivative in the equations of motion to zero — v̇x = v̇y = ṙ = 0 — and solve for the states. The solutions are steady-state cornering equilibria: combinations of speed, side-slip and yaw rate that the car will hold indefinitely for a given steering angle and drive force.

For a given corner, there is usually more than one such solution:

  • A typical cornering equilibrium with small β, both axles operating in their linear region. This is ordinary driving, and it is stable — disturb it and the car returns on its own.
  • One or more drift equilibria with large β, the rear axle saturated and the front axle still holding grip. These describe a sustained power slide — and they are unstable.

The defining signature of the drift equilibrium is counter-steering. In a drift the steering angle takes the same sign as the side-slip angle and the opposite sign to the yaw rate: the car turns right while the front wheels point left. Far from being a corrective reflex, this is a structural property of the equilibrium itself.

Why counter-steer

With the rear axle saturated, it can no longer generate the lateral force needed to balance the yaw moment. The front axle must supply it — and to make force efficiently the front tires have to stay near their grip peak, at a small slip angle. Since the chassis is rotated away from the direction of travel by β, the only way to keep the front tires pointing near their own velocity is to turn them back by roughly that same angle: δ ≈ β. The dramatic opposite lock is simply what "keep the front wheels aligned with the road" looks like from inside a sideways car.

The rear drive force plays the complementary role. Through the friction circle, throttle determines how much lateral capability the rear axle has left, and therefore which equilibrium the car can hold. Steering sets the balance; throttle sets the operating point. This is why drifting is fundamentally a two-input control problem.

6 — Why it is hard: an unstable equilibrium

Linearize the dynamics about a drift equilibrium and examine the eigenvalues of the resulting system. At a normal cornering equilibrium they lie in the left half-plane — the car self-stabilizes. At a drift equilibrium, one eigenvalue crosses into the right half-plane: the point is a saddle.

The practical meaning is immediate. Left alone, a car at a drift equilibrium does not stay there. Small perturbations grow exponentially, and the car departs along one of two failure modes:

  • Spin — side-slip grows without bound and the car rotates out of control.
  • Grip recovery — side-slip collapses back to zero and the drift simply ends.

Holding a drift therefore requires continuous active stabilization. A human drifter does it by feel, at roughly 2–5 corrections per second. An autonomous controller does it explicitly: estimate the state, compare against the target equilibrium, and correct with steering and throttle — fast enough to catch the instability before it grows.

The control problem in one sentence

Drift control is the task of stabilizing an open-loop-unstable equilibrium of a nonlinear system whose actuation is bounded by a friction circle — which is why it makes such a good benchmark for both classical control design and learned policies.

This is the problem AIDrift is built to study. See the Autonomy page for how the estimator and controllers are implemented on the actual car.

7 — Why 1:43 is a real testbed

A fair objection to any scale model: does a palm-sized car actually drift like a real one, or is it merely a toy that slides? The answer comes from dimensional analysis.

For vehicles whose motion is governed by inertia and gravity, dynamic similarity is governed by the Froude number:

Fr = v² / ( g L ) Matching Fr between model and full scale means matching the balance of inertial and gravitational effects — the same regime, at a different size.

Holding Fr constant with a length ratio λ = 1/43 gives the scaling of every other quantity. Velocities scale as √λ, and so do times:

vmodel / vfull = √λ = √(1/43) ≈ 0.15 A car drifting at 15 km/h at 1:43 scale is dynamically equivalent to roughly 100 km/h at full scale.

The consequence for control is the interesting part. Since time also scales as √λ, everything happens about 6.6× faster in wall-clock terms. A correction a full-scale driver would have half a second to make must be made in under 80 milliseconds here. The instability is the same instability — it just arrives sooner, which is precisely why the platform runs a 100 Hz control loop.

What does not scale

Rigid-body dynamics scale cleanly; tires do not. Rubber friction depends on contact pressure, temperature and surface in ways that are not captured by a length ratio, and aerodynamic effects are negligible at this size. Results transfer as dynamic behaviour and control structure, not as quantitative tire predictions — a limitation shared by every scaled vehicle platform.

9 — Further reading

Standard references for the material on this page.

  • H. B. Pacejka — Tire and Vehicle Dynamics. The reference on tire force modelling, including the Magic Formula used to fit the curves in section 3.
  • R. Rajamani — Vehicle Dynamics and Control. Clear derivation of the single-track model, Ackermann geometry, and stability analysis for lateral vehicle dynamics.
  • W. F. Milliken and D. L. Milliken — Race Car Vehicle Dynamics. The standard treatment of steering geometry in competition, including why Ackermann is often reversed at racing speeds.
  • R. Y. Hindiyeh and J. C. Gerdes — "Equilibrium Analysis of Drifting Vehicles for Control Design." Establishes drift equilibria as unstable steady states and derives controllers that stabilize them.
  • E. Velenis et al. — "Steady-State Cornering Equilibria and Stabilisation for a Vehicle During Extreme Operating Conditions." Equilibrium analysis across the full range of cornering conditions, including sustained drift.
  • J. Y. Goh and J. C. Gerdes — "Simultaneous Stabilization and Tracking of Basic Automobile Drifting Trajectories." Extends drift stabilization from a fixed equilibrium to tracking a desired path.
